## Source of the UN text

The text in Part I is the official English text of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, as published by the Office of the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights (source given on that page: United Nations Department of Public Information, New York): <https://www.ohchr.org/en/human-rights/universal-declaration/translations/english>. The organizer compared every article and the Preamble word for word with that page on 2026-11-09 and corrected two small wording differences (Article 3, "the security of person"; Article 21, "the right of equal access"). The UN text has not been shortened, reordered or edited, and the changes of 2026-11-09 (decisions V-08 to V-15) left it alone.

## General reasoning (K23)

*Why this document exists.* Airokrasi puts a human-rights text at the top of its norm order (K1). The founder asked that this text begin from the universal declaration as it stands (K25), so that the project does not pretend to invent human rights and so that anyone can see which words belong to the world and which to Airokrasi. *What it does.* It keeps the 1948 text untouched, marks beside each article what is missing, outdated or disputed, and adds articles for a time in which decisions about people are increasingly prepared by artificial intelligence systems. *Where it comes from.* The Additional Articles build on decision E1 of the Foundations debate (the "Airokrasi Additional Protocol") and on the later debates on corruption, economy and visible state. *What it is not.* It is not a treaty and not yet a decision of the whole project: the draft has not been through the full debate, and the value choices settled by the referee remain open to objection. Its place beside the manifesto and the constitution is an open question (Part III).

# Part I: The UN text with Airokrasi commentary

## Preamble

> **UN text**
>
> Whereas recognition of the inherent dignity and of the equal and inalienable rights of all members of the human family is the foundation of freedom, justice and peace in the world,
>
> Whereas disregard and contempt for human rights have resulted in barbarous acts which have outraged the conscience of mankind, and the advent of a world in which human beings shall enjoy freedom of speech and belief and freedom from fear and want has been proclaimed as the highest aspiration of the common people,
>
> Whereas it is essential, if man is not to be compelled to have recourse, as a last resort, to rebellion against tyranny and oppression, that human rights should be protected by the rule of law,
>
> Whereas it is essential to promote the development of friendly relations between nations,
>
> Whereas the peoples of the United Nations have in the Charter reaffirmed their faith in fundamental human rights, in the dignity and worth of the human person and in the equal rights of men and women and have determined to promote social progress and better standards of life in larger freedom,
>
> Whereas Member States have pledged themselves to achieve, in cooperation with the United Nations, the promotion of universal respect for and observance of human rights and fundamental freedoms,
>
> Whereas a common understanding of these rights and freedoms is of the greatest importance for the full realization of this pledge,
>
> Now, therefore,
>
> The General Assembly,
>
> Proclaims this Universal Declaration of Human Rights as a common standard of achievement for all peoples and all nations, to the end that every individual and every organ of society, keeping this Declaration constantly in mind, shall strive by teaching and education to promote respect for these rights and freedoms and by progressive measures, national and international, to secure their universal and effective recognition and observance, both among the peoples of Member States themselves and among the peoples of territories under their jurisdiction.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** The Declaration is a General Assembly resolution and does not bind by itself. Its language is partly gendered ("man", "mankind"). It was written before digital life. It does speak to "every individual and every organ of society", which today includes those who build, sell and run automated systems; the text does not say so.
- **Later development:** The two Covenants of 1966 (civil and political; economic, social and cultural) gave binding force to most of the rights. The Vienna Declaration of 1993 restated that human rights are universal, indivisible and interdependent. [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** One preamble paragraph for Airokrasi's version: "Whereas these rights apply in the same way when decisions about people are prepared or taken by automated systems, and whereas those who build, order and run such systems are among the organs of society that must respect them;"

> **Reasoning [Preamble commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To state at the start that human rights do not weaken when a machine takes part in a decision, and who is bound.
> - **Why:** The Preamble already addresses "every organ of society"; making this explicit closes the argument that a private builder or a vendor of a system stands outside the Declaration.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated (first proposal).
> - **Source:** `docs/tartismalar/2026-11-08-temeller/09-hakem-karari.md`, E1 (three layers; the human-rights text is the top norm); manifesto draft `docs/tartismalar/2026-11-08-manifesto/02-taslak.md`, Part II "Status".
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 1

> **UN text**
>
> All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights. They are endowed with reason and conscience and should act towards one another in a spirit of brotherhood.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** "Brotherhood" is gendered. "Endowed with reason" has been read by some as making dignity depend on reason, which would wrongly exclude people with cognitive disabilities and invites comparison with machines said to "reason". The text is silent on whether dignity can be measured or scored.
- **Later development:** The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006) states inherent dignity without any test of capacity. [to verify]
- **Proposed addition (a reading note, not a change of wording):** "Dignity is not earned, measured or scored. It does not depend on reason, usefulness or any system's assessment." Derived texts in other languages may render "brotherhood" as "solidarity" or the nearest gender-neutral word.

> **Reasoning [Art. 1 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To protect dignity from being turned into a score or a condition.
> - **Why:** Automated scoring of people (welfare, risk, "reliability") is the most direct way an automated system can treat dignity as measurable.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. Related decision: the manifesto draft, Articles 1 and 2 (no general score of conduct).
> - **Source:** `docs/tartismalar/2026-11-08-manifesto/02-taslak.md`, Articles 1-2; Foundations E1.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 2

> **UN text**
>
> Everyone is entitled to all the rights and freedoms set forth in this Declaration, without distinction of any kind, such as race, colour, sex, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or social origin, property, birth or other status.
>
> Furthermore, no distinction shall be made on the basis of the political, jurisdictional or international status of the country or territory to which a person belongs, whether it be independent, trust, non-self-governing or under any other limitation of sovereignty.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** The list is open ("other status"), but it does not name disability, age, sexual orientation, gender identity, health, genetic features or place of residence. It also does not name the new ground: a profile or score made from a person's data.
- **Later development:** The Covenants repeat the open list. The UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, in General Comment No. 20 (2009), reads "other status" to cover disability, age, nationality, marital and family status, sexual orientation and gender identity, health status, place of residence and economic and social situation. [to verify] Specific treaties cover race (1965), women (1979) and persons with disabilities (2006). The European Convention has a general ban in Protocol 12. The European Court of Human Rights accepts indirect discrimination (D.H. and Others v. Czech Republic, Grand Chamber 2007). [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** After "other status": "such as disability, age, sexual orientation, gender identity, health, genetic features, place of residence, or a profile or score derived from a person's data".

> **Reasoning [Art. 2 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To name the grounds that people actually suffer from today, including the new one made by data.
> - **Why:** Systems learn proxies. A postcode or a purchase history can stand in for ethnic origin or illness. The ground must be named so a person can invoke it.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. The Foundations referee already decided that a ban on unequal treatment by political opinion or profile enters the Additional Protocol (E1).
> - **Source:** Foundations `09-hakem-karari.md`, E1, G4; manifesto draft Article 2; Economy debate Decision 30 (algorithmic favouritism).
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 3

> **UN text**
>
> Everyone has the right to life, liberty and the security of person.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** The text is silent on the death penalty. It is also silent on decisions to take life or liberty made by machines, including autonomous weapons.
- **Later development:** The Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (Article 6) limits the death penalty to "the most serious crimes" and prohibits arbitrary deprivation of life; its Second Optional Protocol (1989) aims at abolition, and the European Convention has Protocols 6 and 13 on abolition. The Human Rights Committee's General Comment No. 36 (2018) interprets the right to life. [to verify] I do not know exactly what General Comment 36 says about autonomous weapons. [to verify]
- **Decided (V-08), in three parts:**
  1. **Machines and liberty.** "No decision to take a person's life or to deprive them of liberty may be taken by an automated system without a human decision."
  2. **The death penalty itself.** Airokrasi does not make abolition a condition of membership, and this commentary does not take a side. It only repeats where international law stands today. Article 6 of the Covenant on Civil and Political Rights allows the penalty only for "the most serious crimes", forbids arbitrary deprivation of life, gives protections for juveniles and pregnant women, and gives anyone sentenced to death the right to seek pardon or commutation. Article 6(6) adds that nothing in the Article may be used to delay or prevent abolition. Each country may abolish the penalty or add stronger safeguards in its own adaptation (K24).
  3. **One rule that belongs to Airokrasi alone.** In a case that may end in a death sentence, nothing produced by an automated system (a recommendation, a classification, a risk or dangerousness score, a referral) may be used as evidence, as a ground, or as an input to the decision. Judicial power in such a case lies wholly with the human judge.
- **What this does not say:** It does not require abolition and does not forbid the penalty. It also does not settle the Human Rights Committee's reading of "most serious crimes" (General Comment No. 36), which should be checked before it is quoted. [to verify]

> **Reasoning [Art. 3 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To close the machine gap on life and liberty, and to say as much about the death penalty as the universal line supports, without going further.
> - **Why:** A death sentence cannot be undone, and an error made with the help of a machine is the worst case: automation bias together with a final outcome. Abolition as a condition of membership would go beyond the Covenant and would shut out countries that ratified it but still keep the penalty.
> - **Debate summary:** Decided by the Value Decision Referee as V-08 (K31, 2026-11-09), which weighed silence, abolition as a condition, and silence plus a specific safeguard, and chose the last. Part 1 follows Foundations decision A1 (item 5). The abolition question is closed by V-08; objection stays open.
> - **Source:** V-08 in `docs/tartismalar/2026-11-09-deger-kararlari/01-hakem-karari.md`; Foundations `09-hakem-karari.md`, A1 item 5, E1, G4, KB-11; Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, Article 6; Second Optional Protocol (1989); European Convention Protocols 6 and 13; Human Rights Committee General Comment No. 36 (2018) [to verify].
> - **Status:** Decided by referee decision (K31), 2026-11-09. Open to objection.

## Article 4

> **UN text**
>
> No one shall be held in slavery or servitude; slavery and the slave trade shall be prohibited in all their forms.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** The modern forms (trafficking, debt bondage, forced labour, forced marriage) are not named. A newer risk: making a right depend on "voluntary" work.
- **Later development:** The Forced Labour Convention of the International Labour Organization (No. 29, 1930) and its 2014 Protocol; the Palermo Protocol on trafficking in persons (2000); Article 8 of the Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** "...including trafficking, debt bondage and forced labour. No right or benefit may be made conditional on compulsory or unpaid work."

> **Reasoning [Art. 4 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To bring the prohibition up to the forms that exist and to block disguised compulsion.
> - **Why:** "Voluntary" work tied to a payment or a right becomes compulsion. The manifesto draft already says that no right may depend on voluntary contribution.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated as a rights text. The Economy debate dealt with voluntary community work (Decisions 10 and 16); I did not re-read their content for this commentary.
> - **Source:** Economy `09-hakem-karari.md`, Decisions 10 and 16; legal note `06-hukuk-notu.md` (International Labour Organization Convention 29 and the European Convention, Article 4).
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 6

> **UN text**
>
> Everyone has the right to recognition everywhere as a person before the law.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** The text does not say who or what is a person. Two live questions: the legal capacity of persons with disabilities, and whether an artificial intelligence system could be given legal personality.
- **Later development:** Article 12 of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006) requires equal recognition of legal capacity, with supported decision-making. The European Parliament's 2017 resolution on robotics asked the Commission to consider a status of "electronic person"; the idea was not taken forward and was widely criticised. [to verify] There is no international agreement on personality for systems.
- **Proposed addition:** "Legal personhood belongs to human beings and to bodies that humans create and answer for. A system is not a person before the law."

> **Reasoning [Art. 6 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To prevent responsibility from sliding onto a system that cannot be held to account.
> - **Why:** A "legal person" machine could absorb blame ("the system decided") and leave the victim without an answerable party.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. Airokrasi has already taken this position in its own architecture.
> - **Source:** Foundations A1 item 10 (agents carry no legal personality; the act is the act of the authorising human organ); manifesto draft Article 6.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 7

> **UN text**
>
> All are equal before the law and are entitled without any discrimination to equal protection of the law. All are entitled to equal protection against any discrimination in violation of this Declaration and against any incitement to such discrimination.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** The text does not say that discrimination can be produced by a system, nor that it can happen without any discriminatory intent.
- **Later development:** The race convention (Article 4) and the Covenant (Article 20, paragraph 2) deal with incitement. Courts have accepted indirect discrimination (see Article 2). [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** "This protection covers discrimination produced by systems, including through data that stands in for a protected ground."

> **Reasoning [Art. 7 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To make equal protection reach automated decisions.
> - **Why:** A system can discriminate with no one intending it; protection must not depend on proving intent.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. See Additional Articles 6 and 7.
> - **Source:** Foundations E1 (protection against algorithmic discrimination), G4 item "language and culture bias audit"; Economy Decision 30.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 8

> **UN text**
>
> Everyone has the right to an effective remedy by the competent national tribunals for acts violating the fundamental rights granted him by the constitution or by law.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** A person often cannot even tell that an automated act harmed them, or how it was made. A remedy that depends on the person proving how a system works is not effective.
- **Later development:** Article 2(3) of the Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (an effective remedy, with enforcement) and Article 13 of the European Convention. [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** "An effective remedy includes the reasons for the act, review by a human being, and, where a system took part, the burden of showing how the decision was made resting on the institution, not on the person."

> **Reasoning [Art. 8 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To make the remedy real for automated acts.
> - **Why:** Experience with automated benefit decisions (the Dutch child-benefit case, the Australian "Robodebt" scheme) shows that errors persist when the person must prove them.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. Follows the Foundations referee's rules that the burden of proof lies with the institution and that the cost of an error falls on the institution.
> - **Source:** Foundations `09-hakem-karari.md`, A1 (reasoning and item 8), H5; manifesto draft Article 10.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 9

> **UN text**
>
> No one shall be subjected to arbitrary arrest, detention or exile.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** "Arbitrary" is not defined. Predictive risk scores raise the new danger of detaining or restricting someone for what a model thinks they may do.
- **Later development:** Article 9 of the Covenant on Civil and Political Rights adds the right to be told the reasons, to be brought promptly before a judge, to challenge detention in court, and to compensation. The Human Rights Committee's General Comment No. 35 (2014) explains that "arbitrary" includes inappropriateness, injustice and lack of predictability. [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** "No one may be arrested, detained or have their liberty restricted on the basis of a predicted risk score or automated assessment. A judge decides, on grounds that can be stated."

> **Reasoning [Art. 9 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To rule out detention by prediction.
> - **Why:** A statistical risk is not an act. It also reproduces past bias.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. A "risk-score ban" is on the list of later waves of the idea pool and has not been discussed.
> - **Source:** Foundations A1 item 5 (liberty restriction is the top human-decision level); `docs/tartismalar/fikir-havuzu-karari.md` ("criminal justice package"); manifesto draft Article 2.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 10

> **UN text**
>
> Everyone is entitled in full equality to a fair and public hearing by an independent and impartial tribunal, in the determination of his rights and obligations and of any criminal charge against him.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** The text does not say that the "tribunal" must be made of human judges. When systems prepare drafts for courts, a person may not know what influenced the decision.
- **Later development:** Article 14 of the Covenant and Article 6 of the European Convention; the Human Rights Committee's General Comment No. 32 (2007). On courts and artificial intelligence, the European Commission for the Efficiency of Justice adopted an ethical charter in 2018. [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** "A tribunal is made of human judges. Systems may help prepare a case but may not decide it, and the parties may know what helped and challenge it."

> **Reasoning [Art. 10 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To keep judgment human and transparent.
> - **Why:** A judgment is the state's strongest act over a person; it needs a person who can be held to account and a reasoning that can be read.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. The Foundations referee set the principle "the verdict belongs to a human judge" (A2, E1).
> - **Source:** Foundations A2, E1 (the right to a human judge); manifesto draft Article 7.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 11

> **UN text**
>
> 1. Everyone charged with a penal offence has the right to be presumed innocent until proved guilty according to law in a public trial at which he has had all the guarantees necessary for his defence.
>
> 2. No one shall be held guilty of any penal offence on account of any act or omission which did not constitute a penal offence, under national or international law, at the time when it was committed. Nor shall a heavier penalty be imposed than the one that was applicable at the time the penal offence was committed.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** Systems that sort people by "likelihood" treat them as probably guilty before any act. The presumption of innocence is written for courts, not for scoring tools.
- **Later development:** Article 14(2) and Article 15 of the Covenant. The European Union adopted a directive on the presumption of innocence in 2016. [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** "The presumption of innocence binds systems too: a statistical risk is not proof and not a reason to restrict a person."

> **Reasoning [Art. 11 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To extend the presumption of innocence to scoring.
> - **Why:** Risk scores are used to rank people for checks and for freedom; they work as a quiet presumption of guilt.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated.
> - **Source:** Manifesto draft Article 2 (no general score of conduct); Foundations E1; `fikir-havuzu-karari.md` (criminal justice package, not yet debated).
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 12

> **UN text**
>
> No one shall be subjected to arbitrary interference with his privacy, family, home or correspondence, nor to attacks upon his honour and reputation. Everyone has the right to the protection of the law against such interference or attacks.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** The text was written before mass data collection, profiling and automated inference. It speaks of "interference", not of the dignity of the data itself.
- **Later development:** Article 17 of the Covenant and Article 8 of the European Convention; the United Nations General Assembly resolution 68/167 (2013) on privacy in the digital age; the Council of Europe data protection Convention 108 (1981, updated 2018) and the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (2016). On surveillance, the European Court of Human Rights (Big Brother Watch v. United Kingdom, Grand Chamber 2021) requires safeguards at every stage, including independent authorisation and review afterwards. In I. v. Finland (2008) it held that a state must make it possible to find out who looked at a patient's records. [Court cases checked in an earlier session against press summaries; the rest to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** "Protection of privacy includes personal data and what is inferred from it. Interference needs a law, necessity, proportion and independent oversight, and the person is told afterwards who looked." (See Additional Articles 8 and 9.)

> **Reasoning [Art. 12 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To bring privacy up to the age of data and to link it to the "who looked" right.
> - **Why:** Aggregated data allows a profile of a whole life. Inferences (illness, politics) are as sensitive as facts.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. Follows the Visible State decisions (the Gaze View) and the Foundations ledger decision (K15: personal data is not kept on the chain).
> - **Source:** Visible State `08-hakem-karari.md`, GD-6 to GD-9; Foundations K15; `docs/tartismalar/2026-11-08-gorunur-devlet/04-hukuk-incelemesi.md`.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 14

> **UN text**
>
> 1. Everyone has the right to seek and to enjoy in other countries asylum from persecution.
>
> 2. This right may not be invoked in the case of prosecutions genuinely arising from non-political crimes or from acts contrary to the purposes and principles of the United Nations.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** The Declaration gives a right to seek and to enjoy asylum, not a right to be granted it. Today, asylum decisions and border checks increasingly use automated tools and biometric databases.
- **Later development:** The Refugee Convention (1951) and its 1967 Protocol set out who is a refugee and ban sending a person back to danger (Article 33); the Convention against Torture (Article 3) bans sending a person to torture. [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** "An asylum claim is decided by human beings. A system may not alone assess a claimant's credibility or risk."

> **Reasoning [Art. 14 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To keep the most dangerous decision for a vulnerable person in human hands.
> - **Why:** An error here can mean return to persecution.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. The Economy referee already placed the right to asylum outside the "ladder of rights" (Decision 53); asylum procedure was left outside that debate.
> - **Source:** Economy `09-hakem-karari.md`, Decision 53; Foundations A1 item 5 (residence and citizenship are top-level human decisions).
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 16

> **UN text**
>
> 1. Men and women of full age, without any limitation due to race, nationality or religion, have the right to marry and to found a family. They are entitled to equal rights as to marriage, during marriage and at its dissolution.
>
> 2. Marriage shall be entered into only with the free and full consent of the intending spouses.
>
> 3. The family is the natural and fundamental group unit of society and is entitled to protection by society and the State.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** This is one of the most disputed articles. "Men and women" and "full age" are not defined. Cultures differ on who may marry whom and on the family. The Foundations debate set three layers for cultural difference (G4): an absolute core, limitable rights with a margin of appreciation, and local matters.
- **Later development:** The Covenant (Article 23) uses the same words. The convention on women (1979, Article 16) demands equal rights in family law and says the marriage of a child has no legal effect. On same-sex couples: the Human Rights Committee found no right to same-sex marriage under the Covenant (Joslin v. New Zealand, 2002); the European Court of Human Rights found no duty to open marriage (Schalk and Kopf v. Austria, 2010) but a duty to offer some legal recognition (Oliari and Others v. Italy, 2015; Fedotova and Others v. Russia, Grand Chamber 2023). [All to verify]
- **Decided (V-09):** Airokrasi sets no extra condition on the form of the couple. It neither requires nor forbids any form; that is for each country to decide in its own adaptation (K24). Three limits stay in place:
  1. **A hard core.** Marriage needs the free and full consent of both spouses. A child cannot marry. Spouses have equal rights in marriage and at its end.
  2. **A ceiling on the country's discretion.** If a country leaves some people out of the legal effects of marriage or family status (inheritance, social security, custody, immigration and family reunification), that exclusion must still respect the ban on discrimination in Articles 2 and 26 of the Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. It needs an objective and reasonable justification.
  3. **No single form of family.** Children born outside marriage and children in single-parent families have the same protection as others (UN text, Article 25(2)). What counts as a "family" may differ from country to country.
- **Airokrasi's own rule:** When a decision fixes family status, custody or family reunification, the system may prepare the case, but a human decides.
- **What this does not say:** It does not require same-sex marriage and does not forbid it. It does not rank any family model above another in morals.
- **Where the universal line stands:** At the universal level the Human Rights Committee found no right to same-sex marriage in the Covenant (Joslin v. New Zealand, 2002), but accepted that refusing social security to an unmarried same-sex partner can be discrimination under Article 26 (Young v. Australia, 2003). In Europe the Court went further for the states bound by the Convention (Schalk and Kopf, Oliari, Fedotova). That regional line binds only those states, and a universal text does not spread it to everyone by itself. [All cases to verify]

> **Reasoning [Art. 16 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To write down what is shared worldwide (the core and the ban on discrimination) and to leave the form of the couple to each country without dropping the ceiling.
> - **Why:** The form of the couple is a field of deep moral disagreement. A universal text that chose a side would narrow who can take part, in either direction; a text with no limit at all would open the door to discrimination.
> - **Debate summary:** Decided by the Value Decision Referee as V-09 (K31, 2026-11-09). Options weighed: leaving the form to the countries with the core only, regulating the form in the universal text, or leaving the form to the countries with a discrimination ceiling. The last was chosen. It rests on the general direction of the universal line, not on the detail of any one case. The question that was left open here is closed by V-09; objection stays open.
> - **Source:** V-09 in `docs/tartismalar/2026-11-09-deger-kararlari/01-hakem-karari.md`; Foundations `09-hakem-karari.md`, G4, E1, KB-11, A1; Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, Articles 2, 23, 26; Convention on women (1979), Article 16; Joslin v. New Zealand (2002), Young v. Australia (2003), Schalk and Kopf v. Austria (2010), Oliari and Others v. Italy (2015), Fedotova and Others v. Russia (2023) [all to verify].
> - **Status:** Decided by referee decision (K31), 2026-11-09. Open to objection.

## Article 18

> **UN text**
>
> Everyone has the right to freedom of thought, conscience and religion; this right includes freedom to change his religion or belief, and freedom, either alone or in community with others and in public or private, to manifest his religion or belief in teaching, practice, worship and observance.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** The right to change religion was disputed when the Declaration was adopted (Saudi Arabia was among the eight states that abstained, among other reasons for this). [to verify] The text is silent on conscientious objection. It was written before technology that reads or shapes mental states.
- **Later development:** The Covenant (Article 18) softens the wording to "to have or to adopt" a religion, makes the inner freedom absolute (no limitation, Article 18(2)-(3) and 4(2)), and allows limits only on manifestation. General Comment No. 22 (1993) accepts conscientious objection as derived from this article; the European Court of Human Rights agreed in Bayatyan v. Armenia (Grand Chamber 2011) and had earlier found Turkey's repeated punishment of an objector to be degrading (Ülke v. Turkey, 2006). [to verify] The 1990 Cairo Declaration on Human Rights in Islam takes a different path. [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** "The inner freedom of thought, conscience and belief is absolute. No one may be compelled to reveal it, and no system may be used to read or alter it."

> **Reasoning [Art. 18 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To confirm the absolute inner core and to protect it from new technology.
> - **Why:** Inference from behaviour and, later, from the body already reveals belief and mood.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. The Foundations referee already decided the inner side of conscience and religion is absolute (G4).
> - **Source:** Foundations `09-hakem-karari.md`, G4; Additional Article 10 below.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 19

> **UN text**
>
> Everyone has the right to freedom of opinion and expression; this right includes freedom to hold opinions without interference and to seek, receive and impart information and ideas through any media and regardless of frontiers.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** The text does not deal with machine-made speech, automated ranking of what people see, or forged media. It has no limits clause, so the limits come from elsewhere.
- **Later development:** Article 19(3) of the Covenant allows limits only if set by law and necessary for rights of others or for security, public order, health or morals; Article 20(2) requires a ban on advocacy of hatred that incites discrimination, hostility or violence. General Comment No. 34 (2011) and the Rabat Plan of Action (2012) explain how to draw this line. [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** "The right to seek and receive information includes knowing whether content was made or ranked by a system. The state does not decide what is true; verification belongs to independent bodies."

> **Reasoning [Art. 19 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To protect free expression while giving people honest signals about machine-made content.
> - **Why:** The Foundations referee set the line: the state does not label true and false, independent civil society verifies, and the ban on forged evidence and fake persons covers measurable facts only, not opinion.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. Follows decision Y2 (Information Environment and Press).
> - **Source:** Foundations `09-hakem-karari.md`, Y2; manifesto draft Article 3.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Article 21

> **UN text**
>
> 1. Everyone has the right to take part in the government of his country, directly or through freely chosen representatives.
>
> 2. Everyone has the right of equal access to public service in his country.
>
> 3. The will of the people shall be the basis of the authority of government; this will shall be expressed in periodic and genuine elections which shall be by universal and equal suffrage and shall be held by secret vote or by equivalent free voting procedures.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

## Article 22

> **UN text**
>
> Everyone, as a member of society, has the right to social security and is entitled to realization, through national effort and international co-operation and in accordance with the organization and resources of each State, of the economic, social and cultural rights indispensable for his dignity and the free development of his personality.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

> **Reasoning [Art. 22 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To make the resource clause answerable to a test and to protect livelihood from automated error.
> - **Why:** The Foundations referee already treats social rights as "target norms" with reasonableness and non-regression review, and decided that livelihood payments continue during review.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. Follows Foundations E1 and the Citizen's Guarantee Package.

## Article 26

> 1. Everyone has the right to education. Education shall be free, at least in the elementary and fundamental stages. Elementary education shall be compulsory. ## Article 27

> **UN text**
>
> 1. Everyone has the right freely to participate in the cultural life of the community, to enjoy the arts and to share in scientific advancement and its benefits.
>
> 2. Everyone has the right to the protection of the moral and material interests resulting from any scientific, literary or artistic production of which he is the author.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** Paragraph 1 gives everyone a share in the benefits of science. Paragraph 2 protects authors. Artificial intelligence puts the two against each other: the gains of systems trained on a great deal of human work, and the interests of those who made that work. The Declaration does not say how to balance them. I do not offer a solution here.
- **Later development:** Article 15 of the Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights; General Comment No. 17 (2005) (authors' interests are human rights but not the same as intellectual-property rules) and No. 25 (2020) (science). [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** Paragraph 1 is applied in Additional Article 12 (a share in the common gains). Paragraph 2, for the case where systems are trained on creators' work, is applied in Additional Article 15 (creators' interests). That Article also answers who decides: the legislature writes the mechanism, an independent human judge or body settles disputes, and an artificial-intelligence system cannot decide a dispute about its own training.

> **Reasoning [Art. 27 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To connect "benefits of science" to the Citizen's Dividend and to Additional Article 12, and to point from the authors' paragraph to Additional Article 15.
> - **Why:** If systems gain from human work, the question of who benefits is a rights question, not only an economic one. The two paragraphs pull against each other and have to be weighed, not one declared absolute.
> - **Debate summary:** The conflict that this commentary first left open was settled by the Value Decision Referee as V-10 (K31, 2026-11-09), which added Additional Article 15 and gave this commentary a cross-reference to it. The link to Additional Article 12 comes from V-11.
> - **Source:** V-10 and V-11 in `docs/tartismalar/2026-11-09-deger-kararlari/01-hakem-karari.md`; Economy Decision 1; manifesto draft Article 18; Additional Articles 12 and 15.
> - **Status:** Decided by referee decision (K31), 2026-11-09, through Additional Article 15. Open to objection.

## Article 29

> **UN text**
>
> 1. Everyone has duties to the community in which alone the free and full development of his personality is possible.
>
> 2. In the exercise of his rights and freedoms, everyone shall be subject only to such limitations as are determined by law solely for the purpose of securing due recognition and respect for the rights and freedoms of others and of meeting the just requirements of morality, public order and the general welfare in a democratic society.
>
> 3. These rights and freedoms may in no case be exercised contrary to the purposes and principles of the United Nations.

**Airokrasi commentary (proposal)**

- **Gap or dispute:** "Morality, public order and the general welfare" are open words that governments have used to justify much. Paragraph 3 can be misused to silence critics. "Duties to the community" must not become a price for rights.
- **Later development:** The Covenant replaces the general clause with specific, narrower limits that must be "necessary" (for example Articles 12(3), 18(3), 19(3), 21, 22(2)). The Siracusa Principles (1984) and the European Convention's test of "necessary in a democratic society" add proportionality. [to verify]
- **Proposed addition:** "Limits must be set by an accessible and foreseeable law, be necessary and proportionate, and be open to review by an independent court. A limit is not applied by a system alone without human review in the individual case. Duties to the community are not a condition for receiving a right."

> **Reasoning [Art. 29 commentary]**
> - **Purpose:** To tighten the limits clause and stop it being used as a lever.
> - **Why:** Automated enforcement of vague limits (content removal, benefit sanctions) multiplies the harm of broad wording.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. Follows the Foundations decision on rights conflicts (D3) and the three layers (G4).
> - **Source:** Foundations `09-hakem-karari.md`, D3, G4, A1; manifesto draft Article 19 (no right depends on voluntary contribution).
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

# Part II: Airokrasi Additional Articles

**Status.** These articles are entirely an Airokrasi proposal and are not part of the UN text. They add to the Declaration and never lower it: where an Additional Article and a right in the UN text or the two Covenants seem to conflict, the higher protection applies. The first group (Articles 1 to 8 and 11) builds on decision E1 of the Foundations debate (the "Airokrasi Additional Protocol"). The Foundations referee's decision is a draft awaiting the founder's approval (including KB-11). Additional Articles 12, 13, 15 and 16 come from the Value Decision Referee's decisions of 2026-11-09 (V-10 to V-12 and V-15); they are written in two layers where the Article says so: a core a person can enforce in court, and a part that is realized step by step. "System" here means any automated or artificial-intelligence system that prepares or takes part in a decision about people; Airokrasi's own name for its "agents" is still being discussed (K26).

## Additional Article 1: The right to know who or what you are dealing with

Everyone has the right to know whether they are dealing with a person or with an automated system, in every contact with a public authority and wherever a system takes part in a decision about them.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 1]**
> - **Purpose:** To end the hidden machine.
> - **Why:** People answer, trust and argue differently when they know who is on the other end.
> - **Debate summary:** The Foundations referee added "the right to know one is dealing with an agent" to the Additional Protocol (E1). Not yet debated as a rights text.
> - **Source:** Foundations `09-hakem-karari.md`, E1; manifesto draft Article 9.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Additional Article 2: The right to a reason

Everyone affected by a decision has the right to be told its reasons in plain language: which facts and rules were used, and whether a system helped to prepare it. A decision without reasons that can be stated is not a valid ground to limit a right.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 2]**
> - **Purpose:** To make decisions answerable to the person.
> - **Why:** Without reasons there is nothing to challenge. Complex models make this harder, so it has to be written as a right.
> - **Debate summary:** Named in E1 ("right to a reason"). Not yet debated in this wording. Open point: how much explanation a complex model can honestly give.
> - **Source:** Foundations E1; A1; manifesto draft Article 9; Visible State GD-9 (a record states its limits).
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Additional Article 3: The right to a human

Everyone has the right to bring any decision that affects them to a human being who can change it. The last stop of every appeal is a human judge or a human body. No one is bound by an automated decision alone when it affects liberty, family, nationality, livelihood, health or punishment.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 3]**
> - **Purpose:** To guarantee a human at the end of every road.
> - **Why:** Automated errors spread fast and cannot be argued with. The cost of error falls on those least able to bear it.
> - **Debate summary:** Named in E1 ("right to appeal to a human"). The Foundations referee ranked actions by harm (A1) and added the digital-divide guarantee (H5). Not yet debated in this wording.
> - **Source:** Foundations A1, E1, H4, H5; manifesto draft Articles 9, 10 and 20.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Additional Article 4: The right to a human judge

Everyone has the right to have their rights and obligations, and any criminal charge, decided by human judges who are independent and impartial. Systems may help prepare a case but may not decide it, and the parties may know what helped and may challenge it.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 4]**
> - **Purpose:** To keep judgment human.
> - **Why:** A judgment is the state's strongest act over a person and needs someone who can be held to account.
> - **Debate summary:** Named in E1 ("the right to a human judge"). Not yet debated in this wording.
> - **Source:** Foundations A2, E1; manifesto draft Article 7.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Additional Article 5: Someone answers

Systems are tools and have no legal personality. Every act of a system is attributed to a named human office that answers for it. "The system decided" is no excuse, and a person who was harmed does not have to show how a system works in order to be put right.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 5]**
> - **Purpose:** To make sure a human is always answerable.
> - **Why:** When many parts (builder, vendor, operator, user) share a task, each can point to another; the victim is left with no one to ask.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated as a rights text. It carries decision A1 item 10 and principle I8 into the Declaration.
> - **Source:** Foundations A1 item 10; manifesto draft Articles 6 and 8.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Additional Article 6: Protection against algorithmic discrimination

No one may be discriminated against by a system, whether directly or through other data that stands in for a protected ground. Systems that take part in decisions about people are tested for such discrimination before use and while in use, by someone independent of their maker, and the results are public.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 6]**
> - **Purpose:** To give the equality rule a working check on systems.
> - **Why:** Systems learn from past decisions and repeat their bias; the harm is large and hidden.
> - **Debate summary:** Named in E1. The Foundations referee decided that models are checked for language and culture bias (G4). Not yet debated in this wording.
> - **Source:** Foundations E1, G4, I7; Economy Decision 30 (algorithmic favouritism).
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Additional Article 7: No treatment by profile

No one may be given a general score of their conduct, character or worth, and no right or service may depend on such a score. No one may be treated worse because of political opinion, or because they did not vote for those in power.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 7]**
> - **Purpose:** To rule out the "social score" and political punishment through services.
> - **Why:** Services are the easiest lever for pressing a voter. A general score turns every small act into a lever.
> - **Debate summary:** E1 adds the right "not to be treated according to a political opinion or profile (including a ban on discrimination against those who did not vote for the government)". The Economy debate asked that the general ban on scores be checked against E1 (Decision 52).
> - **Source:** Foundations A2 item on protecting opposition voters, E1; manifesto draft Article 2; Economy Decision 52, T10.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Additional Article 8: Data dignity

Personal data belongs to the person it describes. The state and others say what they hold, why, and for how long. Correction and erasure are not lost because of how records are kept. What is inferred about a person is personal data too. Data collected for one purpose is not combined with data for another without a law that says so.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 8]**
> - **Purpose:** To give data a status of dignity, not only of property or consent.
> - **Why:** Combined data builds a picture of a whole life. A ledger that cannot forget would make erasure empty.
> - **Debate summary:** Named in E1 ("data dignity"). The Foundations referee decided personal data is not kept on the chain and only fingerprints and metadata are (K15). Legal opinion: a fingerprint linked to a person can itself be personal data.
> - **Source:** Foundations E1, K15 and KB-9; Visible State GD-7 and GD-8; legal review `04-hukuk-incelemesi.md`; manifesto draft Article 4.
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Additional Article 9: The right to know who looked

Everyone has the right to see who looked at their personal data, in which role, when and for what purpose, in a record that cannot be altered. Where the law delays or withholds this for investigation or security, it does so only for a limited time, under independent authorisation and oversight, and the person is told afterwards. A record that shows nothing does not prove that no one looked, and the record says so.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 9]**
> - **Purpose:** To let people check the people who check them.
> - **Why:** Unauthorised looking is only deterred if people can find it. The European Court of Human Rights held in I. v. Finland (2008) that this is a part of effective protection.
> - **Debate summary:** The Visible State referee decided on the Gaze View (a record kept by each system, a pull view for the citizen, an independent auditor; no central vault, no "citizen key"). Details of delayed notification are for a separate security wave of laws.
> - **Source:** Visible State `08-hakem-karari.md`, GD-6, GD-9, GD-11; legal review `04-hukuk-incelemesi.md`.
> - **Status:** Draft. The security exceptions are not yet decided.

## Additional Article 10: Freedom from manipulation

Everyone has the right to form their thoughts and choices free from manipulation. Persuasion that aims at a person's known weaknesses, forged evidence, fake persons or sources, and the hidden use of systems to shape opinion or votes are forbidden. Opinion, comment and open argument remain free, and the state does not decide what is true.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 10]**
> - **Purpose:** To protect the freedom to think from the new power of tailored persuasion.
> - **Why:** Experiments reported in the manifesto draft show conversational systems can move political views, and persuasion gets stronger as accuracy falls. Tailoring to the individual removes the shared public debate.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated as a rights text. The Foundations referee limited the ban to measurable facts (forged documents, fake persons or sources, profile-based persuasion that targets weakness), so that it cannot become political censorship (Y2).
> - **Source:** Foundations Y2; manifesto draft Article 3 and Part I ("persuasion machines are coming").
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Additional Article 11: Participation without digital means

No right or service is lost, delayed or made harder because a person is offline, cannot use digital tools or chooses not to. A human channel exists for every public service at the same standard, and it reaches people where they live and in their own language.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 11]**
> - **Purpose:** To prevent a digital divide from becoming a rights divide.
> - **Why:** Older people, rural communities and the poor are least online and most dependent on public services.
> - **Debate summary:** Named in E1. The Foundations referee decided on the same service standard for every channel, a Citizen's Desk (Yurttaş Masası) and a "Lost Citizen Report" (H5).
> - **Source:** Foundations E1, H5; manifesto draft Article 20; Visible State GD-11 (item 5).
> - **Status:** Draft, open for discussion.

## Additional Article 12: A share in common gains

1. Everyone has the right to a livelihood. No one may be left without the means to live decently, and a livelihood guarantee is a right, not a favour.

2. The wealth that automation and artificial intelligence create from common resources, knowledge and public data is shared under a country's own law. That share, the Citizen's Dividend, is paid regularly under rules written in advance, to the people the law names, and cannot be raised or cut at the whim of those in power. Stopping a payment is an adverse decision: a human reviews it, and payment goes on while it is appealed. Where the law treats people differently, for example by nationality, it needs a justification that is objective, reasonable and proportionate.

*Two layers.* A person can go to court for: payment under the written rule; human review before a payment is stopped; equal treatment and fair procedure; and the minimum core of a livelihood, which belongs to everyone. The level of the dividend, the share of common wealth set aside for it, and the payment calendar are realized step by step. They are judged for reasonableness and against going backwards, and a court cannot fix an amount where there are no resources. Those who apply the norm may not use social rights as a tool to block the budget or a service.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 12]**
> - **Purpose:** To prevent gains of automation from collecting in a few hands, and to say clearly what a person can claim in court and what is realized over time.
> - **Why:** The founder expects job loss and wants fair distribution (K10, K13). A rule that is a right, not a favour, also protects the payment from becoming a political tool. A livelihood guarantee and a dividend from common wealth are not the same thing: the first is already in the UN text (Article 25) and in the Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights and belongs to everyone; the second is an order a country sets up, and its law says who is entitled.
> - **Debate summary:** The Economy referee decided the Citizen's Dividend (paid from a fund with rules, gates and a protection against raising it before elections). The founder chose that the dividend goes to citizens only (Economy `13-kurucu-onayi.md`, item 1). The first draft of this Article had spoken of "every resident adult", which contradicted that choice; V-11 corrected it by splitting the Article into the universal guarantee and the country's dividend. V-11 also answered the earlier open question: the Article has a directly enforceable core and a target-norm layer. The split does not justify differences based on nationality. If a country makes them, they need an objective and reasonable justification, and the legal risk is tracked as R-01 (the Gaygusuz and Andrejeva line of European case law). [to verify]
> - **Source:** V-11 and V-07 (2) in `docs/tartismalar/2026-11-09-deger-kararlari/01-hakem-karari.md`; Economy `09-hakem-karari.md`, Decisions 1, 2, 6, 13 and 22; Economy `13-kurucu-onayi.md`, item 1; Foundations E1; manifesto draft Article 18; Article 27 of the UN text (share in scientific benefits); Article 25 of the UN text; Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, Article 26; Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, Articles 2, 9 and 11; Committee General Comments No. 3 and No. 20 [all to verify].
> - **Status:** Decided by referee decision (K31), 2026-11-09. Open to objection. Entitlement to the dividend is a matter of each country's law and remains under watch for discrimination risk (R-01).

## Additional Article 13: Future generations

People not yet born have a claim on the world they will inherit. Decisions that cannot be undone, such as lasting harm to climate, water, soil, living things or public debt, are weighed against their interests, and their effects are stated openly before the decision.

When the irreversible load of a proposed law passes a threshold set in law, three things are required. A Future Statement (Gelecek Şerhi) of the effects is prepared and published. A Citizens' Panel reads the proposal a second time and its opinion is published. Parliament then decides by a named vote with written reasons. A law above the threshold that has no Future Statement cannot be promulgated, and the Constitutional Board (Anayasa Kurulu) can review whether this procedure was followed.

*Two layers.* Procedure is a right a person can enforce: the duty to explain and assess effects before an irreversible decision, the named vote and Panel reading above the threshold, review of the procedure, and access to information. The weight that the interest of future generations carries in the end is realized step by step and is left to the judgment of the lawmaker. A court sets aside a policy on its merits only when it is manifestly unreasonable or a step backwards. Those not yet born cannot sue, so a country may give standing to qualified bodies or to the Citizens' Panel. The right to a healthy environment in Additional Article 16 works together with this Article.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 13]**
> - **Purpose:** To put those who cannot vote into the weighing, without taking the final word from the elected parliament.
> - **Why:** Four-year terms and sealed promises for one term can favour the short run. Some harms arrive decades later. A body with a power to suspend laws would sit above the elected parliament and would clash with the lottery panel's role as an advisory body (Foundations A3); the chosen route makes the future visible and puts a name and a reason on every vote instead.
> - **Debate summary:** Decided by the Value Decision Referee. V-12 split the idea-pool proposal FA-08: the Future Ledger (Gelecek Defteri) and the Future Statement were accepted; a "Future Council" of young citizens chosen by lot with a binding six-month suspension power was rejected as it stood. The Ledger is the national accounting of irreversible loads (carbon budget, public debt, groundwater, soil, biodiversity, waste). Its list of indicators is written in law and can only be widened by a qualified majority, and the Statistics Institute computes it. The Statement is written by an independent, neutral agent with at least two independent methods and states ranges of uncertainty, not point estimates; the Model and Technical Audit Boards check the methods. The threshold comes from the Ledger indicators. The Panel is not limited by age. No qualified majority is asked of Parliament, and no lottery body can suspend a law. V-11 gave the Article its two layers. V-15 added the cross-reference to the healthy-environment right. The question of a direct right or a target norm and the question of a mechanism, both open earlier, are closed.
> - **Source:** V-11, V-12 and V-15 in `docs/tartismalar/2026-11-09-deger-kararlari/01-hakem-karari.md`; `docs/tartismalar/fikir-havuzu.md`, FA-08; `fikir-havuzu-karari.md`; Foundations `09-hakem-karari.md`, A3, B3, K17; Economy Decisions 50 and 57; Visible State GD-12 and GD-18; the Welsh Well-being of Future Generations Act (2015) and the Finnish Committee for the Future, taken from the idea pool [to verify].
> - **Status:** Decided by referee decision (K31), 2026-11-09. Open to objection. The referee would reopen the Future Council idea only on a new argument that resolves the clash with A3 or on a shadow-period measurement showing the chosen route falls short.

## Additional Article 14: Children in the digital environment

Children have the right to special protection from profiling, manipulation and the commercial use of their data. Systems that affect children are designed for the children's best interests, and children and their parents or guardians are heard in line with the child's age.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 14]**
> - **Purpose:** To protect those least able to protect themselves.
> - **Why:** Children cannot give meaningful consent and carry the effects of early profiling for decades.
> - **Debate summary:** Not yet debated. The Visible State debate already held back showing records for children and protected persons until special rules are written (GD-11).

## Additional Article 15: Creators' interests

The moral and material interests of creators whose works are used to train artificial-intelligence systems are protected by law. The law gives them at least these three things. First, they can find out which of their works were used. Second, they have an effective and accessible way to object to the use or to claim payment. Third, there is either a fair mechanism for a share or payment, or a clearly written and proportionate counterweight, such as scientific research or the public interest. Each country decides the form of the mechanism in its own law: prior permission, a right to opt out, collective payment or another. A dispute is decided by an independent human judge or body. An artificial-intelligence system cannot decide a dispute about its own training.

Airokrasi's own public models are the first to be bound by this Article. The Common Public Model Infrastructure publishes a summary of the data its models were trained on.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 15]**
> - **Purpose:** To handle the clash between creators' interests (UN text, Article 27(2)) and everyone's share in the benefits of science (Article 27(1)) without forcing one mechanism on every country, and without leaving the clash unnamed.
> - **Why:** Artificial-intelligence systems are trained on a very large amount of human work, and the gains can be collected apart from those who made it. The same gains are also the subject of society's right to share in science. Three minimum guarantees are simpler than one imposed mechanism and can be adapted. The word "counterweight" could be read very broadly and weaken the protection, so the Article ties it to written grounds and to a judge's review. The state could not ask others to meet a duty it does not meet itself, which is why its own public models come first.
> - **Debate summary:** Decided by the Value Decision Referee as V-10 (K31, 2026-11-09). Options weighed: silence, a strong right (prior consent or compulsory payment), and principles with minimum procedural guarantees. The strong right was set aside because it hurts the right to share in science and goes beyond the many different copyright systems; silence was set aside because it would leave the clash unnamed. Who decides: the country's legitimate lawmaker writes the principle and the mechanism, an independent human judge settles disputes, and an artificial-intelligence system cannot decide a dispute about its own training. No independent evidence was found on how well payment and opt-out mechanisms work, so the mechanism is left to each country. The format and minimum content of the training-data summary is a new research task.
> - **Source:** V-10 in `docs/tartismalar/2026-11-09-deger-kararlari/01-hakem-karari.md`; Economy `09-hakem-karari.md`, Decisions 1 and 22; Foundations `09-hakem-karari.md`, B4 and D3; UN text, Article 27; Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, Article 15; Committee General Comments No. 17 and No. 25; European Union Artificial Intelligence Act, Article 53 (training-content summary and copyright policy for providers of general-purpose models) [all to verify].
> - **Status:** Decided by referee decision (K31), 2026-11-09. Open to objection.

## Additional Article 16: The right to a healthy environment

Everyone has the right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment.

Some parts of this right can be enforced by a person: access to information about the environment; taking part in the decisions that affect it; an effective remedy; and a public statement of the environmental effects of a decision before it is taken. These work together with Additional Article 13 and the Future Statement. The right itself, that is, the quality of the environment that people can expect, is realized step by step. It is judged by the rule against going backwards and by a test of reasonableness.

The environmental cost of the state's own artificial-intelligence systems (energy, water, hardware and waste) is measured and published as a separate item in the Airokrasi balance sheet. A component for which this table cannot be written does not go into operation.

> **Reasoning [Additional Article 16]**
> - **Purpose:** To put the environmental right into the rights text, and to make the state's own artificial-intelligence footprint visible.
> - **Why:** Damage to the environment cannot be undone and is passed on to future generations. The infrastructure of artificial intelligence itself uses energy and water. The text names no figures, because this repository holds no measurement of those uses; it says "measure and publish" and ties going into operation to the table.
> - **Debate summary:** Decided by the Value Decision Referee as V-15 (K31, 2026-11-09). Options weighed: not included, the Manifesto only, the Declaration with a short reference in the Manifesto. The Manifesto alone was set aside because the project's rule is that rights are written only in the Declaration; leaving it out was set aside in view of the General Assembly resolution and the project's own footprint. The Manifesto gets a short value article that refers here and does not repeat the text. In the constitution it is a principle, not part of the unamendable core, because there is no binding global treaty yet and the content is still developing. The procedural part follows the two-layer approach of V-11.
> - **Source:** V-15 in `docs/tartismalar/2026-11-09-deger-kararlari/01-hakem-karari.md`; Manifesto critique `04-elestiri.md` (web check of the General Assembly vote); `fikir-havuzu.md`, FA-08; Foundations `09-hakem-karari.md`, E1; Economy `09-hakem-karari.md`, Decision 58; Additional Article 13; UN General Assembly resolution A/RES/76/300 (28 July 2022; 161 in favour, none against, 8 abstentions; not binding); Human Rights Council resolution 48/13 (2021); Inter-American Court of Human Rights Advisory Opinion OC-23/17; European Court of Human Rights, KlimaSeniorinnen (2024); International Court of Justice advisory opinion on climate change (2025); Aarhus-type procedural guarantees [all to verify].
> - **Status:** Decided by referee decision (K31), 2026-11-09. Open to objection.

# Part III: Open questions for the debate

1. **Place of this document.** Foundations E1 sets three layers: the full texts of the Universal Declaration and the two Covenants; the interpretation by their bodies; and the Airokrasi Additional Protocol. Part II would be a draft of the third layer. Part I commentaries have no legal force unless the founder decides so. The relation to the manifesto and to the constitution is open (task definition, point 5).
2. **Membership condition (KB-11).** If joining requires accepting the Covenants and the Additional Protocol, states that did not ratify them stay outside. This is a value choice the founder has not yet confirmed.
3. **Death penalty (Article 3).** *Closed by V-08.* No abolition condition; the commentary relays the international line; no output of an automated system may be used in a death-penalty case.
4. **Form of the couple (Article 16).** *Closed by V-09.* Airokrasi sets no extra condition; the hard core, the discrimination ceiling and the openness of the family stay.
5. **Authors and training data (Article 27).** *Closed by V-10.* Additional Article 15 gives three minimum guarantees; the mechanism is left to each country and a human decides disputes.
6. **Direct right or target norm (Additional Articles 12 and 13).** *Closed by V-11.* Both are written in two layers: an enforceable core and a target-norm layer. Additional Article 12 also separates the universal livelihood guarantee from the country's Citizen's Dividend.
7. **Future generations (Additional Article 13).** *Closed by V-12.* Future Ledger and Future Statement accepted; Future Council with a power to suspend rejected as it stood; a Panel second reading and a named vote instead. (V-15 adds Additional Article 16 beside it.)
8. **Commentary on the seven untouched articles.** Still open. Reviewers are invited to propose commentary on Articles 5, 13, 15, 17, 24, 25 and 28.
9. **Wording of the UN text.** Closed in substance on 2026-11-09: the organizer compared the text in Part I word for word with the official OHCHR page and corrected two small differences. A new reader should still be able to check it against that page.
